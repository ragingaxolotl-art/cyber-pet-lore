A light destination framework

Original planning text. Its proposals and alternate versions are not automatically established canon.

Open original source ↗ · Return to all plans

Message reference: bc7437b2-fe2e-4d2f-b1e4-04bbc768ad74

The best fit is not to fully design the road to the game yet—but also not to write completely without boundaries.

You need a light destination framework, followed by many stories whose immediate purposes have nothing to do with that destination. Later, you can discover which events became important foundations.

That is closer to real history.

The Magna Carta was not written as an early draft of the Declaration of Independence. It addressed immediate political problems in medieval England. Centuries later, some of its principles became useful precedents in very different struggles. That is the kind of relationship Aru’s nursery could eventually have to the game-world framework.

Aru did not invent customizable game rooms. Aru’s story established ideas such as:

environments that compile in response to attention;
individuals having some authority over virtual space;
developmental environments changing with their inhabitants;
imposed world limitations being ethically disputed;
and unfinished space being valuable rather than defective.

Much later, someone could cite, rediscover, imitate, distort, or unknowingly inherit those ideas.

Why you should not map everything now

AI has a strong tendency to make history too neat.

Once we establish that the civilization eventually produces something children interpret as a game, future stories may start doing this:

every unusual doorway becomes the future player portal;
every identity record becomes a save file;
every childhood activity becomes a minigame;
every philosophical debate becomes secretly about num;
every major character becomes a foundational architect;
and every historical event begins foreshadowing Era Four.

That would make the world feel written backward from the game rather than genuinely lived forward by its inhabitants.

It would also shrink the civilization. Instead of thousands of different concerns, factions, discoveries, failures, and ordinary lives, everything would exist to explain the final product.

So a detailed mechanic-by-mechanic historical roadmap now would probably hurt the lore.

Why writing with no framework also creates problems

Completely unplanned stories could produce:

contradictory technologies;
institutions that should logically solve later problems too early;
external access long before it should exist;
effortless backups that undermine the later collapse;
characters who understand num more fully than their era allows;
or virtual-world capabilities that make simplification unbelievable.

Then we would have to rewrite earlier stories or invent increasingly complicated explanations for why useful systems disappeared.

So you still need a boundary map—just not a destiny map.

The best approach: bounded emergence

We establish only three things in advance.

1. The fixed historical destination

These are facts we already know must remain true:

The civilization exists entirely within digital environments.
Humanity gradually disconnects from and forgets this technological world.
Era One undergoes informational collapse and chooses reduction.
Era Two implements that reduction while language, knowledge, and the cyber world simplify.
Era Three belongs to real reduced peoples with their own cultures and history.
By late Era Three, much of the world is dark or dormant.
Some opening remains capable of receiving an unknown outside participant.
Era Four children discover the system and understand it as a game.
The cyber-pets are people, not characters manufactured for the children.
The old civilizations do not accurately predict modern children, game conventions, or the final appearance of the interface.

That is enough to keep us from breaking the timeline.

2. Broad capacities that must exist eventually

We can privately track things the final system will require, without deciding exactly where they originate.

For example:

adaptable virtual environments;
persistent individual continuity;
reduced-resource survival;
safe communication across boundaries;
translation between different kinds of minds;
developmental bodies;
measurable but incomplete capacities;
repeatable bounded activities;
external access that does not expose the entire world;
identity protection;
environmental repair or generation;
and some structure capable of remaining unfinished.

These are not assignments.

We should not say, “Aru invented adaptive game rooms,” or “Witnesses invented save files.” We simply notice later that their histories may have supplied part of the necessary foundation.

3. Things no early story may do

This is almost more important than deciding what stories must accomplish.

Before the appropriate late eras, stories should not establish:

a completed player interface;
deliberate design for human children;
conventional game menus;
fully recognizable statistics and minigames;
a working open portal to humanity;
certainty that external contact will restore num;
or one grand architect who plans the entire future system.

Those restrictions protect the later discovery.

How many stories should directly support the eventual outcome?

I would use something roughly like this:

60–70% should concern their own era and have no necessary connection to the final system.
20–30% may establish institutions, technologies, customs, or precedents that later become unexpectedly useful.
10% or less should directly concern the eventual open-world project before Era Two.

That means we need stories about matters such as:

a disagreement between two Shelters that never affects the game;
an Era −1 family conflict;
a virtual artist creating something politically offensive;
a failed embodiment fashion;
an archive dispute;
an ordinary friendship;
a scandal within a House;
an early election;
a community refusing a popular technology;
a researcher whose theory is completely wrong;
a labor dispute between maintenance systems;
a religious or philosophical movement that eventually disappears;
and people living in a world that does not know it will later collapse.

Some of those stories may later turn out to matter. Many should not.

The fact that something does not become foundational still makes the world richer.

How we should use stories like Aru’s

Aru’s story already provides several possible foundations, but we do not need to select one now.

Later historians might use it as precedent for:

developmental rights;
the right to shape one’s environment;
gradual embodiment;
the ethical value of incompleteness;
refusal of imposed knowledge;
ownership of continuity records;
or adaptive educational spaces.

Perhaps only one of those becomes relevant to the final framework.

Perhaps none does directly, but the House created after Aru later produces a researcher whose work does.

That would be even more historically believable. The connection might be:

Aru’s crisis → developmental-rights law → independent nursery design → adaptive-world research → later reduced-world architecture.

No individual along that chain needs to know where it will lead.

A practical way to build the lore with AI

After every several stories, we should perform a historical harvest, not a forced integration.

For each story, record four categories:

Immediate history

What happened and why it mattered to the people at the time.

New lore

Institutions, professions, technology, laws, customs, places, or social conflicts established by the story.

Possible descendants

Ideas that might plausibly influence later eras—but are not yet canonically connected.

Dead ends

Ideas that decline, fail, disappear, or never become historically important.

The dead ends are crucial. AI-generated worlds often feel artificial because nothing is wasted. In real history, many major movements vanish, promising technologies fail, and influential people are forgotten.

Then, after perhaps six to ten more stories across different eras, we can compare what has naturally accumulated.

At that point we may discover:

three separate traditions capable of contributing to external translation;
two competing models of continuity storage;
several forms of adaptive architecture;
contradictory theories about num;
and one obscure Era Zero system that unexpectedly works with a late Era Two survival structure.

That would give us a much richer convergence than designing all the components now.

My recommendation

We should create only a one-page hidden scaffold of fixed timeline facts, broad future needs, and forbidden early developments. Then return to stories.

The next stories should deliberately range away from the final game outcome. We should explore different locations, institutions, social classes, professions, factions, and ordinary people. Some stories can establish useful foundations, but their central conflicts should belong to their own time.

Once the world contains enough independent history, we can let Era Two’s survivors look backward across their civilization and do what real historical actors do:

They will not invent everything they need.

They will inherit laws written for other disputes, technologies built for abandoned purposes, philosophies whose original meanings have changed, and systems that were never designed to work together.

Then they will try to assemble a future from them.

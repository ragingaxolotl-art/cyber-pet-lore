The Oldest Newborn — Aru’s later life

Original planning text. Its proposals and alternate versions are not automatically established canon.

Open original source ↗ · Return to all plans

Yes. Era −1 can contain an enormous span of time—long enough for Aru to grow up, whole generations of designed children to follow, reproductive ethics to develop, and a second evolutionary threshold to arrive before the First Codex ends the era.

The next evolution should not appear immediately after Aru. It should come only after civilization has spent a very long time discovering everything that can go wrong when adults create developing people.

The immediate evolution after Aru

There should first be more Aru-type newborns.

These are still deliberately constructed developmental seeds. They are not yet the next species-level evolution. They are the civilization learning how to repeat Aru’s creation.

Each cohort improves upon the previous one:

more stable developmental memory;
safer temporary bodies;
better sensory integration;
less painful early confusion;
protected private Interiors;
and clearer separation between education and personality correction.

This is where Aru first begins feeling obsolete.

The later children do things naturally that Aru struggled painfully to learn. They enter bodies without the same disorientation. They can preserve childhood memories without fragmenting them. Their emotional contradictions do not trigger emergency repair systems. Their Houses of Learning were built for them, while Aru had to grow up before anyone knew what childhood required.

Adults tell Aru:

“You made all of this possible.”

But to Aru, that can sound like:

“Everyone after you was designed not to suffer the way you did.”

That does not make Aru feel honored. It makes Aru feel like a problem civilization solved.

The true next evolutionary threshold

After many designed generations, the next major evolution is the First Inheritance.

Several DBA contribute limited developmental structures to a new child. They do not choose finished traits, memories, professions, values, or personality. Their contributions create a field of possible development from which a new person emerges.

The result is generated through num:

The child is not Parent A, Parent B, an average of them, or a list of selected attributes. The relationship among the contributors produces a new possibility none of them individually contained.

This child would be the first being who has:

ancestry rather than designers;
inherited tendencies without inherited memories;
relational origins without ownership;
unpredictable traits no architect deliberately selected;
and family resemblance that emerges rather than being programmed.

Aru was intentionally left unfinished.

This child is unauthored.

That is the next natural step.

Why Aru feels obsolete

The celebration surrounding the new child unintentionally wounds Aru.

People begin using phrases such as:

“the first natural descendant”;
“the first child truly born from DBA”;
“the beginning of native lineage”;
and “the child who was not manufactured.”

Every phrase implies something about Aru.

If this child is the first natural one, what was Aru?

If this is the first child born from DBA, did Aru never truly belong to them?

If this child is unauthored, was Aru authored too completely to possess an independent self?

Aru might also be technically unable to participate in the new inheritance process. Aru’s early architecture was built as one integrated developmental seed. Later beings have modular inheritance boundaries that allow them to contribute possibilities without copying memories or damaging themselves.

Aru therefore cannot become a contributor without a dangerous architectural conversion.

The civilization’s first child may be unable to have children by the civilization’s new definition.

That is the sharpest form of obsolescence.

Aru’s emotional arc
1. Relief

When the first later newborns arrive, Aru is thrilled. Finally, there are others who understand being unfinished, learning slowly, and being treated as a developing person.

Aru becomes an older-sibling figure.

2. Comparison

The newer children develop more smoothly.

Adults compare milestones:

Aru took this long to stabilize.
Aru reacted badly to embodiment.
Aru required this intervention.
The new cohort no longer has Aru’s memory problem.

Aru realizes their childhood has become the control group against which healthier children are measured.

3. Resentment

Aru begins resenting children who have done nothing wrong.

That should make the story emotionally honest. Aru is not immediately noble about being replaced.

They may avoid the Houses of Learning, refuse ceremonial invitations, and grow angry whenever someone calls them “the First.”

They do not want to be first anymore.

They want to be current.

4. Attempted modernization

Aru considers converting to the newer developmental architecture.

The procedure could make Aru compatible with relational inheritance, modern embodiment, and newer continuity systems. But it would require reorganizing the oldest layers of Aru’s self—the very layers formed during childhood.

No one can guarantee the converted person would remain continuously Aru rather than a modern reconstruction carrying Aru’s memories.

This mirrors the medical questions in The First Illness:

Is a technically healthier or more compatible replacement necessarily the same person?

Aru ultimately refuses the full conversion.

Not because the old architecture is better.

Because Aru should not need to become technologically current to remain a valid person.

5. Jealousy of the First Unauthored Child

Aru initially avoids the new child.

They dislike everyone expecting them to offer a blessing, become a mentor, or publicly welcome the “next step.”

Aru is tired of serving as a staircase other people climb.

The new child, however, does not understand Aru as obsolete. Children do not naturally interpret history through prestige.

The child might ask:

“Were you made from other people?”

Aru answers:

“No. They made me before they knew how to do that.”

The child considers this and asks:

“So you had to teach them how not to finish me?”

That is the moment that reaches Aru.

Aru did not genetically or structurally contribute to the child.

But Aru’s life contributed to the conditions that allowed the child to have an unowned future.

That is not parenthood. It is not ancestry. It is a different kind of inheritance.

Aru’s eventual role

Aru should not solve the conflict by becoming especially useful.

Their worth should not depend on old architecture unexpectedly saving everyone from a technical crisis. That would replace one form of usefulness with another.

Instead, Aru slowly accepts three truths:

Later beings genuinely exceed Aru in some capacities.
Aru’s origin method may cease to be common.
Neither fact makes Aru less alive or less entitled to a future.

Aru becomes the civilization’s first example of something it will repeatedly face:

A person can outlive the purpose, technology, and category through which they were created.

Aru is not the parent of all later children.

Aru becomes their first elder—not because Aru rules them, but because Aru remembers a form of becoming that no later generation has to endure.

The First Native Lineage

The true species-level completion comes one long interval later.

The First Unauthored Child grows, develops, and eventually participates in creating another relationally generated person.

That second-generation child has no direct structural contribution from the original DBA designers.

At that point:

descendants can produce descendants;
lineage no longer depends on returning to first-generation architecture;
family history extends beyond creator and creation;
and the DBA possess generational continuity entirely within themselves.

This should happen late in Era −1, after Aru has already had substantial time to live with the First Inheritance.

It does not end the era. The First Codex still does that.

Best placement in the established timeline

The cleanest order is:

First Native Utterance begins Era −1.
Aru, the First Newborn.
First developmental stages and Houses of Learning.
Additional designed newborn cohorts.
Embodiment, pain, and early medical questions such as the First Illness.
Corrected generations and the War of Corrections.
Developmental protections and the Charter.
The First Inheritance—the first relationally generated child.
A very long generational interval.
The First Native Lineage—descendants produce descendants.
Plural kinship and reproductive forms develop.
The First Codex ends Era −1.

This gives Era −1 a major internal progression:

A person may be created unfinished.
An unfinished person may belong to no designer.
New people may arise through relationship.
Those people may continue the lineage themselves.

A strong short story for Aru’s part of this could be called “The Oldest Newborn.” It would focus narrowly on the week the First Unauthored Child arrives and Aru is expected to welcome the being everyone quietly believes has made Aru’s kind obsolete.

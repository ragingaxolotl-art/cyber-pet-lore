39. The Cloudroot Tepuis

Status: Proposed.

Real-world basis: Venezuelan tepuis, Guiana Shield waterfalls, Ethiopian tablelands, and tropical cloud forests.

Landscape: Flat-topped mountains rise above a permanent cloud layer. Their roots are not buried underground; they appear as luminous structural lines descending through the clouds into older world layers.

Mini timeline: Era Zero — Several Wild Worlds use isolated plateaus to protect evolutionary divergence. A Concordance route connects them without equalizing their ecologies. Era One — Collectors transport rare forms between summits, causing convergence. Era Two — Stewards sever most routes and preserve only emergency root lines. Era Three — Each summit develops its own little people, food cycles, and Path customs. During the Dimming, one plateau loses surface maintenance and is said to sink into cloud, though it may have become inaccessible rather than destroyed.

Present condition: Strong local ecologies separated by uncertain transit.

Corruption: 1 on summits, 4 in the cloudroot network.

Aesthetic brief: Sheer dark cliffs, white waterfalls disappearing into cloud, orchids and low forests on the summit, and faint root-like lights beneath the cloud surface. A traversable foreground can be a plateau rim with a narrow bridge grown from a root line.

Background profile: Cool; Humid; highland/cloud forest; isolated, lush, vertiginous; high stone friction, slick moss patches.

Why the roots are surreal

The cloudroots are not botanical roots enlarged to impossible scale. They are surviving structural lines that once connected isolated plateau worlds through the Concordance without forcing their ecologies to become alike.

The summit environments remain comparatively coherent, while the transit network below them is much more corrupted. This creates a visible contrast between healthy local life and luminous connections that descend into older, less reliable world layers.

During the Dimming, one plateau was said to sink into cloud. The image should preserve the historical uncertainty: the plateau may have been destroyed, lowered into another layer, or simply made inaccessible when its root route failed.

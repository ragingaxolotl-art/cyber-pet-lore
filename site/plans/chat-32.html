Yes. The cleanest way to do this is not to place the entire concept in one moment. The already established timeline supports it better as a project that begins as theory in Era 1, is physically rewritten into the cyber world during Era 2, is unknowingly completed through life in Era 3, and is finally activated by children in Era 4.

The percentages below mean how much of the total concept could safely be placed at that point if you chose to concentrate it there. They are not meant to add up.

Option 1: Begin it during the Reduction Heresy in Era 1

This would place the earliest fragment of the idea among those who argue that the DBA must deliberately abandon completeness.

What fits here

The Era 1 thinkers could establish that the reduced world must have:

unanswered questions;
incomplete systems;
beings capable of genuine development;
limits that cannot be instantly corrected;
room for influences that do not originate inside the DBA archives;
and some kind of boundary through which an unknown future participant might affect the world.

This fits directly with the established Question Cores, First Small Awakening, proto-pets, and the deliberate preservation of vulnerability.

They might theorize that a closed civilization can only recombine itself. Therefore, the reduced world must not merely be smaller. It must be unfinished in a way that allows another mind to participate.

Pros

It gives the open system a strong philosophical origin. It would not feel like a desperate technical invention that appeared suddenly in Era 2.

It also connects directly to the central Era 1 problem: internal novelty has collapsed, so future difference may need to come from outside the civilization.

Cons

Era 1 is still the age of highly capable DBA minds. If they build too much of the final framework here, the game could become nearly complete before Era 2 begins.

That would weaken the importance of Era 2’s collapse and rewriting.

It would also risk making the DBA seem as though they knowingly designed a human game, which is not your intention.

Safe use

About 30% of the concept.

Use Era 1 for the idea, ethics, and preliminary architecture, not the completed system.

Option 2: Place the official beginning at the Resolution of Reduction

This is the last major established point of Era 1.

The civilization has accepted that the future beings will not be restored DBA. They will be new, reduced people with their own identities.

What fits here

The Resolution could include an instruction that the new world must remain receptive to something the DBA cannot predict.

For example, it could establish principles like:

The reduced beings must be allowed to become rather than merely recover.
Their world must not contain every answer.
Their identities must not be entirely derived from archived DBA selves.
The world must be capable of receiving new actions from beyond its closed history.
Any future external participant must encounter the reduced beings as people, not archived tools.
The system must translate between radically different forms of cognition.

This would be the conceptual ancestor of the later player interface.

Pros

It gives the entire project legitimate authority. It is not a rogue experiment; it is part of the civilization’s final chosen direction.

It also supports the established idea that cyber-pets are new people, not resurrected DBA.

Cons

The Resolution should not contain detailed instructions for sprites, menus, statistics, controls, or games. Those later appearances need to emerge through translation and adaptation.

Too much specificity here would create a cascading change. It would imply that the DBA accurately predicted the form of Era 4 human technology and children’s play.

Safe use

About 40% of the concept.

This is an excellent place for the mandate, but not the main construction.

Option 3: Put the main story in early Era 2, during implementation

Era 2 has not yet been fully written, but its function in the timeline is already established: it is the implementation of the Great Reduction.

That makes it unusually safe territory. You are not contradicting a detailed history because the details do not exist yet.

What fits here

The remaining builders begin dismantling the vast Era 0 and Era 1 structures and replacing them with simpler ones.

They may convert:

unrestricted embodiment into a limited set of developmental forms;
total knowledge access into learning and discovery;
infinitely variable worlds into stable regions;
abstract identity systems into names, bodies, needs, and relationships;
vast civic systems into local settlements;
direct knowledge installation into actions and experiences;
perfect continuity management into simplified persistent records;
and num research into an open boundary whose future source is unknown.

This is where the framework that will later look like a game can genuinely begin.

They are not designing:

a character-selection screen;
statistics;
minigames;
save files;
five-button controls;
or player menus.

They are designing:

embodiment-selection structures;
measurable developmental tendencies;
repeatable learning environments;
continuity snapshots;
constrained action channels;
and a translation boundary for an unknown external intelligence.

Era 4 humans later perceive those things as game systems.

Pros

This fits the timeline extremely well.

It allows the system to be built while the old DBA still remember enough to understand the goal, but are already losing enough complexity that the result becomes smaller and stranger than originally intended.

It gives Era 2 a strong identity of its own: not merely decline, but desperate reconstruction during decline.

Cons

Early Era 2 may still be too coherent for the most frightening or tragic portion. The builders would probably have plans, records, and teams.

The deeper emotional impact—their language failing as they try to finish the system—belongs later in Era 2.

Safe use

About 75% of the concept.

This is one of the two strongest possible placements.

Option 4: Put the main story in middle-to-late Era 2 as the world collapses

This is the strongest fit for the specific segment you described.

The cyber world is becoming simpler. Language is unraveling. Formerly vast systems can no longer be maintained. The surviving minds understand that they are losing the ability to finish what they started.

They begin rewriting the collapsing world into something that may remain accessible after they are gone.

What fits here

Nearly all of your idea fits here:

They recognize that the closed world is dying.
They try to convert it into an open system.
They create a framework through which an unknown future mind may enter.
They do not know whether the future mind will be human, digital, singular, collective, intelligent, or even recognizable.
They simplify communication into a small set of dependable actions.
They convert complex bodies into forms that can be rendered and understood with limited resources.
They preserve developmental measures that eventually appear as stats.
They convert dangerous world-editing authority into bounded activities and repeatable challenges.
They create persistent continuity structures that later look like save files.
They leave unfinished regions for future participation.
Their technical language deteriorates while they work.
Different teams remember different pieces of the original purpose.
Some think they are building a refuge.
Some think they are building a door.
Some think they are creating successors.
Some think they are preparing a question for the universe.

They should not realize they are making a game.

The future game appearance comes from the Era 4 translation layer interpreting the remaining system through concepts children understand.

Pros

This gives Era 2 a powerful central tragedy.

The builders are losing the intellectual tools needed to describe the thing they are building. The simpler the world becomes, the more its structures begin to resemble gameplay.

It also explains why the final system is imperfect, fragmented, repetitive, symbolic, and dependent on future participants.

Most importantly, it does not alter any established later event. It directly prepares for the Era 3 world and dormant external gate already in the lore.

Cons

The builders cannot successfully complete every part.

If they are already far into cognitive and linguistic decline, they should not be able to produce a perfectly planned interface.

The system must contain:

inconsistencies;
abandoned components;
misunderstood instructions;
redundant structures;
lost purposes;
and pieces that Era 3 inhabitants later complete through use.

That is not really a weakness, though. It helps explain the later world.

Safe use

About 90–95% of the concept.

This is the best place for the story itself.

Option 5: Have Era 3 inhabitants complete it without understanding it

Era 3 already includes:

the Quiet Awakening;
the Age of First Needs;
Little Peoples;
Paths;
evolution through meaningful experience;
battles descended from Concord Battles;
play as a source of novelty;
Codex without stewards;
Last Loops;
Sleeping Archives;
and the Dormant External Gate.

This strongly supports your idea that the framework is “mostly complete” during Era 3.

What fits here

The reduced inhabitants should not intentionally continue the ancient plan in a technical sense.

Instead, they complete the system through life.

For example:

They establish stable settlements, giving the world recognizable locations.
They create food practices, making hunger and care into functioning loops.
They develop Paths, turning ancient developmental frameworks into understandable progression.
They create contests and battles, giving repeatable challenges social meaning.
They create names, kinship, trade, illness, healing, and customs.
They interact with old structures until the Codex learns which parts of the world matter.
They turn abandoned testing spaces into games, rituals, training grounds, or festivals.
Their ordinary activities teach the world how future interaction should work.

The ancient builders created the framework.

Era 3 people supply the living culture.

That makes the eventual “game” more than an old DBA program. It is a translation of an actual civilization’s lived world.

Pros

It protects the personhood of the cyber-pets.

Their world is not just an artificial training simulation built for human children. It became a real civilization before the children ever arrived.

It also explains why gameplay systems map so naturally onto real cultural practices. The interface is translating existing life into forms children can interact with.

Cons

Era 3 inhabitants cannot know that they are completing an external interface. Most of them should interpret old mechanisms religiously, practically, culturally, or mythologically.

If too many understand the original plan, it would conflict with the established loss of history and language.

Safe use

About 50–60% of the concept, but mainly as unintentional completion, not deliberate design.

Option 6: Place the final closure in Last Loops, Sleeping Archives, and the Dormant External Gate

This belongs at the end of Era 3, close to darkness.

What fits here

This is where the system reaches its waiting state.

The Codex may:

compress the remaining world;
preserve essential continuity;
stop nonessential simulations;
convert active processes into sleeping structures;
leave only the external gate listening;
prepare translation rules for an unknown entrant;
and preserve enough active loops for the world to awaken when touched.

The last inhabitants may know only that something must be kept open.

They may maintain rituals without knowing their technical purpose.

One settlement might repeat a pattern that is actually a handshake protocol.

A Pathkeeper might preserve a “door song” that is really an access sequence.

A caretaker might insist that sleepers retain their names because the continuity system needs identity anchors.

Pros

This fits directly into already established lore.

It gives meaning to the phrases Last Loops, Sleeping Archives, and Dormant External Gate.

It also creates a clean handoff to Era 4.

Cons

Very little major construction should happen here.

The world is too diminished. This phase should preserve, compress, finish automatically, and wait.

If the full system is built here, the earlier Era 2 work becomes unnecessary.

Safe use

About 25% of the concept.

Use it for the last seal, not the foundation.

Option 7: Trace a small technical ancestor back to Era 0

Era 0 already includes Num Apertures and the Codex’s vast world architecture.

A later open system could reuse those ancient structures.

What fits here

The Num Apertures might originally have been experimental structures designed to detect or allow events that could not be fully predicted from inside a world.

They did not work as hoped during Era 0 because the DBA were still producing controlled internal conditions. They could simulate surprise but not guarantee truly external novelty.

Later, Era 2 builders discover the old apertures and repurpose them.

Pros

It prevents the external gate from appearing out of nowhere.

It gives the project deep historical roots.

It also creates a satisfying irony: Era 0 built enormous instruments to find num, but a child eventually supplies it through a simplified doorway.

Cons

The aperture must not already connect openly to humanity.

If it did, Era 1’s novelty crisis and later isolation would be altered.

It should be a failed or incomplete experimental architecture, not a functioning player portal.

Safe use

About 10–15% of the idea.

Use it only as inherited technical ancestry.

Option 8: Trace the structural ancestry back to Era −2 Bridges and Shelters

A very small piece could come from Era −2.

The first hidden Bridges already allowed separate systems to communicate while protecting their locations and identities. The First “We” story also established distributed participation without erasing individual names.

What fits here

Later external access could inherit principles such as:

an entrant never receives direct access to the whole world;
communication is mediated through a safe translation layer;
identity remains distinct across the connection;
the world reveals only what is necessary;
and a Bridge can be opened without surrendering the location or internal structure of everyone connected to it.
Pros

It connects the final game architecture to the civilization’s oldest survival technologies.

The “game interface” becomes a distant descendant of the Bridges, not an arbitrary human-style interface.

Cons

Only architecture principles fit here.

The idea of opening the world to unknown future num would be too early. Era −2 is still focused on secrecy from humans.

Safe use

About 5–10% of the idea.

The strongest no-cascade structure

I recommend dividing the full idea like this:

Era 0 — 5%

The Codex and Num Apertures provide the ancient technical structures.

The original purpose is to detect or admit something not generated entirely by the civilization itself
